2.1 What Is AI, Generative AI, and LLM?

Before we start using AI tools, let’s first understand what we actually mean by “Artificial Intelligence.”

The term covers many ideas, from a simple phone assistant to tools like ChatGPT that can write essays or code. Think of this section as building blocks — each concept adds another layer of understanding.

2.1.1 Artificial Intelligence (AI)

Artificial Intelligence (AI) means using computers to do tasks that normally require human thinking.
For example:

  • When Netflix recommends a movie you might like.
  • When your phone unlocks after recognizing your face.
  • When Google Maps finds the fastest route to school.

In all these examples, the computer is using data — what it has seen before — to make a smart choice.

AI is the broadest term that covers all kinds of “smart” technologies we use daily.

✏️ Simple definition: AI is when computers are designed to mimic human thinking — by learning from data or experience and then using that to make decisions, predictions, or even generate new content.

2.1.2 Machine Learning (ML)

Inside the big world of AI, there is a smaller part called Machine Learning. Machine Learning means teaching computers to find patterns and improve with experience — just like how people learn from practice.

Example:

  • A spam filter in your email learns which messages are spam after looking at thousands of examples.
  • A music app learns what songs you like and recommends similar ones.

✏️️ Simple definition: Machine Learning helps computers learn automatically from data, without being told every single rule.

2.1.3 Deep Learning (DL)

Deep Learning is a special kind of machine learning that uses something called neural networks — an innovative way of organizing the computer’s learning process (Yep, they are called “neural” because they mimic how neurons in our brain signal one another !).

A deep learning algorithm is a type of neural network that has more than three layers — including input, hidden, and output layers. This idea is shown in the diagram below:

Source: IBM Data and AI Team (2024), AI vs. machine learning vs. deep learning vs. neural networks.

The “deep” in deep learning refers to the depth of layers in a neural network. This helps it recognize complex patterns like faces, voices, or handwriting.

Example:

  • Your phone’s camera can recognize your pet in pictures.
  • Speech-to-text programs like Siri or Alexa understand spoken words.

✏️ Simple definition: Deep Learning is a more advanced type of machine learning that helps computers recognize very complex patterns — like images or voices.

2.1.4 Generative AI (GenAI)

Now comes Generative AI, the kind that can create things.

Instead of just identifying or predicting, this type of AI can generate new content — text, images, music, or code — based on what it has learned.

Examples include:

  • ChatGPT: writes text or code.
  • DALL·E: creates new images from text prompts.
  • Google Gemini or Copilot: helps summarize, write, and brainstorm ideas.

✏️ Simple definition: Generative AI can create new things — like essays, pictures, or programs — by recognizing patterns in huge amounts of data.

Think of it like this:
If you’ve read hundreds of stories, you could probably able to write one that sounds similar — even if it’s completely new. That’s what Generative AI does — it learns the style and structure, then creates something new from it.

2.1.5 Large Language Models (LLMs)

Large Language Models (LLMs) are a type of GenAI focused on text. These models are trained on an enormous amount of text — from books, websites, articles, and code — to learn how people use language.

So take ChatGPT for example, “GPT” stands for Generative Pre-trained Transformer:

  • Generative: It creates (generates) new text.
  • Pre-trained: It was trained on a massive amount of text before you ever use it.
  • Transformer: This is the specific kind of AI model it uses — great at understanding language.

✏️ Simple definition: LLMs are advanced programs that predict what to say next by recognizing language patterns from huge amounts of text.

LLMs don’t think or understand like humans. They simply predict what word (or sentence) should come next, based on patterns they’ve seen before. LLMs are very good at sounding confident — but the models don’t “understand/know” things.

Sometimes they can make up information that sounds right but isn’t (this is called “hallucination”). That’s why it’s important to double-check facts using reliable sources or walk through the logic yourself.

2.2 Limitations and Ethics of Using GenAI

As you begin using AI tools in your learning and daily work, it is important to think carefully about what AI can and cannot do.

Generative AI is a powerful tool for writing, coding, and problem solving, but it is not a human brain. It does not truly understand language or meaning — it simply imitates what understanding looks like by recognizing patterns in data.

🔍 Not a Human Brain
AI systems mimic reasoning but do not actually think, understand, or know the truth. They produce text or code by predicting what is likely to come next based on what they have seen in their training data.
AI can sound intelligent because its responses are often fluent and well-structured, but this fluency does not mean understanding. Unlike people, AI does not experience the world, form opinions, or evaluate meaning — it only predicts.

2.2.1 Limitations of AI

Even though Generative AI can produce impressive results, it has clear limits that students must keep in mind:

  • Can generate wrong or made-up information (“hallucinations”).
    Sometimes AI invents facts, names, or citations that sound believable but are not real.

  • Lacks real-world context unless you provide it.
    It only knows what is written in your prompt; it cannot “guess” what you mean.

  • Can repeat social or data biases.
    Because AI learns from human data, it may repeat stereotypes or biased language found in its training material.

  • Does not reason or understand ideas.
    It imitates the structure of language, but it cannot truly analyze meaning or intent.

In short, AI is a tool for generating patterns — not a system that understands truth or logic. You should always be the one interpreting, questioning, and verifying what it produces.

2.2.2 Ethical Use of AI in Learning

When using AI in academic or professional settings, ethical use means using it to support your own thinking, not to replace it.

Here are some basic guidelines for responsible use:

  • Always fact-check AI-generated answers.
    Verify statistics, quotes, and claims using trusted academic or official sources.

  • Never submit AI output as your own work.
    Passing off AI-generated writing as personal writing is a form of plagiarism.

  • Use AI for learning, not shortcutting.
    Ask it to explain, summarize, or suggest, but make sure you still think critically about the result.

  • Remember: AI does not “know” — it predicts.
    You are responsible for the accuracy, originality, and integrity of any work that includes AI assistance.

Using AI responsibly means developing judgment — understanding when it helps you think and when it might mislead you.

2.2.3 Why Can AI Generate Things So Well (Even Without Thinking)?

You might wonder how AI can produce such fluent text or realistic answers even though it does not think. The reason lies in how these systems are trained.

  • AI systems like Large Language Models (LLMs) are trained on massive amounts of text and data from books, websites, and code. They learn probabilities; given a prompt, they predict which words or phrases are most likely to come next based on patterns in the training data. Because of their size and exposure to so much language, they can mimic human-like writing or reasoning very convincingly.

  • However, they do not understand meaning or reasoning in the way humans do. They have no goals, beliefs, or consciousness; they simply select the next most likely token (word or symbol). This is why their answers can appear smart and fluent, yet sometimes are inaccurate, inconsistent, or shallow in reasoning.

AI tools can sound like they understand things because they’re very good at predicting what comes next in language or code, but they are not thinking beings. They imitate patterns, rather than reasoning like us.

LLMs are like a 12-year-old who has read every classic novel, wiki entry, and Reddit thread — in every language, ever exist — and is trying to sound smart like an adult. But they don’t actually understand what any of it means…. Now imagine that 12-year-old is a robot who doesn’t feel and experience the world like we do… — Chen (2025), Attempt to teach LLMs in POLI 105

2.3 “Vibe Coding”

Recently, a new term has been making the rounds in tech and programming circles: vibe coding. This idea is exciting and has caught public attention because it seems to promise a future where we can “skip” learning to code and just tell the computer what to do.

🔍 Vibe Coding
It describes an AI-assisted coding style where someone explains what they want in natural (plain) language, and the AI generates most of the code. Instead of typing every line manually, you might guide the AI, test what it creates, and refine the result until it works.

For example, someone might say: “Make a dashboard that shows unemployment trends,” and the AI would generate code automatically. But while this sounds exciting, it is also important to understand what it does—and why starting with “vibe coding” is not the best way to learn programming.

We strongly discourage jumping straight into vibe coding without first learning the fundamentals of how a programming language works — just like most data scientists, researchers, and teachers do.

Gen AI can certainly help you learn a language step by step, but the most effective approach is to use it as a learning partner, not as a replacement for learning.

To learn more about the trend, see:

  • Vibe Coding: Collins Dictionary’s Word of the Year 2025 (CNN)

2.3.1 Why You Should Still Learn to Code

Even though AI can write or explain code, learning to code yourself is essential. Understanding the basics of syntax, logic, and data flow gives you control over what the AI generates and helps you think critically about how code works.

  • Understand what AI produces.
    Knowing the language helps you evaluate AI-generated code and recognize errors or inefficient solutions.

  • Avoid “copy–paste AI” work.
    Without a foundation in coding, you risk relying entirely on AI-generated scripts that you cannot explain or maintain.

  • Build real problem-solving skills.
    Coding teaches you structured thinking and debugging—skills that AI can support, but not replace.

2.3.1.1 Why Explore Vibe Coding? And When?

Once you have some coding experience, exploring AI-assisted coding can be valuable.
It can make your workflow faster and more creative when used properly.

  • Speed and prototyping.
    AI can help generate starting points quickly, allowing you to test ideas and adjust your code faster.

  • Accessibility.
    Even beginners can create simple projects by describing them in plain language.

💡 Learning to code gives you the foundation to use AI effectively and responsibly. Instead of depending on AI, you’ll learn to collaborate with it. The key is balance: learn the basics first, then use AI tools to expand what you can do more efficiently.

2.4 GenAI-Assisted Coding: Using GenAI as a Learning Tool

Although we do not recommend starting with vibe coding as a beginner, Generative AI can be a low-cost, customizable, and interactive way to learn programming. You can use AI to ask questions, fix errors, or clarify coding concepts—especially when you are working in R for this course.

The goal is to use GenAI to understand code, not to replace the process of writing it.

2.4.1 How GenAI Coding Assistants Work

  • GenAI coding tools are powered by large language models (LLMs) trained on millions of lines of code and text.
  • They can generate, complete, or explain code snippets based on your instructions.
  • They do not understand code like humans do; instead, they predict what code “should” follow from your input.

These tools are best used as interactive tutors—helping you learn what the code means and how to fix it.

2.4.2 How to Use GenAI Effectively When Learning to Code

Here are some effective strategies for using GenAI in your coding practice:

  • Ask “why,” not just “how.”
    Instead of asking “Give me R code to make a plot,” try:
    “Explain what each line in this R code does.”

  • Debug smarter.
    If you get an error, copy the message into GenAI and ask:
    “What does this error mean in R?” or “How can I fix it while keeping my code simple?”

  • Translate coding concepts.
    Ask AI to summarize what a function like geom_bar() or filter() does—in plain English.

  • Learn by comparison.
    Try: “Can you show me another way to write this R code?”
    This helps you see that there are often several valid approaches to a problem.

2.4.3 ⚠️ Cautions

  • Do not ask GenAI to write an entire script for you. Use it to explain, clarify, or debug code you already wrote.
  • Always review and test AI’s suggestions; even correct-looking code can be wrong or inefficient.
  • Use GenAI as a learning partner, not a shortcut. Focus on understanding why something works — not just repeatedly prompting to “make it work for me.”

3.0.1 Why Storytelling Matters

When we see a chart, our eyes naturally search for patterns — like differences between groups, upward or downward trends, or surprising spikes.

3.1.1 Communicate Clearly with Simple, Effective Visuals

A good visualization should make your message easy to understand — even for someone seeing it for the first time.
Your goal isn’t to show off R skills, but to help others quickly grasp what the data means.

Here are some simple but powerful tips:

  • Use clear titles and subtitles. Tell your reader what to look for.
    • Instead of “GDP vs. Democracy,” try:
    • “Countries with higher GDP per capita tend to have higher democracy scores.”
  • Include context and captions. Add a short note under your plot that tells the reader:
    • What the data shows (e.g., a trend, comparison, or relationship)
    • The source (e.g., V-Dem v15, World Bank)
    • The time period (e.g., “1990–2022”)
    • Any special notes (e.g., “Small island states excluded due to missing data”)

🔍 Example caption:
Source: World Bank (2024). GDP per capita, constant 2015 USD, 1990–2022. Scale begins at zero.

  • Keep it simple and readable.
    • Avoid 3-D effects, overly bright colors, or extra gridlines.
    • Use clear fonts and good color contrast (e.g., blue vs. orange).
    • Always label axes and include units (e.g., “GDP per capita (USD, thousands)”).
    • Start numeric axes at zero unless you have a good reason not to — otherwise, small differences might look exaggerated.

💡 A great plot is one that people can understand in under a minute — even without extra explanation. Think of yourself as a storyteller. Your data is the evidence, and your chart is how you communicate that evidence clearly and credibly to others.

Here’s a simple formula to keep in mind:

Question → Data → Chart → Takeaway

A good chart/plot can be summarize into one clear takeaway sentence, such as:

  • Countries with higher democracy scores tend to have greater gender equality.
  • From 2000 to 2020, voter turnout declined in nearly every region.
  • Despite similar income levels, Country A and Country B have very different levels of press freedom.

3.2 When a Chart Doesn’t Work Well

Now let’s revisit the pie chart example and reflect Why This Is Not a Good Visualization. Even though this chart runs without any coding errors, it’s not very helpful for understanding the data. Let’s think about why it fails to tell a clear story.

  • The chart doesn’t match the data type.
    The variable IGO_count represents the number of intergovernmental organizations that each country belongs to. Turning that count into a percentage of a circle doesn’t make sense — the values are not parts of one whole, but separate totals for each country.

  • Too many categories.
    A pie chart works best when there are fewer than five or six categories. Here, we have many countries, so each slice becomes tiny and hard to read. The viewer can’t easily tell which country has more or fewer IGOs.

  • Color and shape overwhelm rather than help.
    The color legend is long and visually overwhelming.

  • No clear takeaway.
    What do we actually learn from this chart? It doesn’t show a trend, a comparison, or a relationship — just a collection of similar-looking slices.

3.2.1 Lesson from Bad Visualization

Not every type of data fits every kind of plot.
When choosing a visualization, we should always match it to:

  1. The type of data — whether it’s categorical, numerical, or time-based, and
  2. The purpose — what we want to show, compare, or explain.

🔍 Reflection:
Suppose you want to show the population percentage of each country in Europe.

Would a scatterplot be useful here? Probably not. Scatterplots are best when we have two continuous (numeric) variables — for example, GDP per capita on the x-axis and Democracy Score on the y-axis. Each point represents a pair of values for one observation. These plots are useful for showing relationships or correlations, not proportions or categories.

What about a pie chart?
A pie chart can work, but only if you have a few categories (e.g., 5–6 countries). If you try to include all the countries in Europe, the pie will be filled with so many tiny slices that it’s impossible to read.

When you have many categories, it’s usually better to use a bar chart, because bars make it easier to compare values side by side.

3.3.1 Pie Chart — Showing Proportions

A pie chart shows how different categories make up parts of a whole. Each slice represents a proportion or percentage of total observations.

What It Is

  • A pie chart divides a circle into slices that represent each category’s share of the total.
  • It’s best for showing simple composition summaries — not detailed numerical comparisons.

Type of Data

  • Nominal or categorical variables (e.g., region, IGO participation level, income group).

Purpose

  • To compare relative proportions among a few categories (usually fewer than six).
  • To provide a quick overview of how the data is distributed across groups.

3.3.1.1 Example with ggplot2: IGO Participation in Sub-Saharan Africa (2014)

Let’s visualize the proportion of IGO participation levels among Sub-Saharan African countries in 2014.

This pie chart shows how many countries fall into each participation level — Low, Medium, or High — based on how many intergovernmental organizations they belonged to.

We’ll use dataset POLI_LIT_v1 dataset and aggregate the data into categories that work well for visualizing proportions.

# Pie Chart- IGO Participation in Sub-Saharan Africa (2014)
# Create a subset for the region (e_regionpol_6C == 6) in 2014
data <- POLI_LIT %>%
  filter(e_regionpol_6C == 4, year == 2014)

# Summarize by participation level
summary_latin <- data%>%
  count(IGO_participation_level) %>%
  mutate(prop = n / sum(n) * 100)

# Make a pie chart of proportion by participation level
ggplot(summary_latin, aes(x = "", y = prop, fill = IGO_participation_level)) +
  geom_col(width = 1) +
  coord_polar(theta = "y") +
  labs(
   title = "IGO Participation Levels in Sub-Saharan African Countries, 2014",
    subtitle = "IGO Memberships(count), Low (≤50), Medium (51–70), High (≥71)",
    caption = "Source: POLI_LIT_v1 (2025)",
    y = "Participation Level",
    x = ""
  ) +
  scale_fill_viridis_d(option = "cividis") +
  theme_minimal()

# ggplot() starts the plot and defines what data and variables to use.
# aes() maps variables: y determines slice size; fill gives each category a color.
# geom_col() builds proportional bars from the data values.
# coord_polar(theta = "y") converts bars into a circular pie chart.
# labs() adds the title, subtitle, and legend label.
# scale_fill_viridis_d() uses a color-blind-friendly palette.
# theme_minimal() keeps the chart clean and easy to read.

3.3.1.2 Why This Chart Works Better than the Previous One

Compared to the earlier “bad example,” this pie chart communicates its message clearly:

  • The variable fits the chart type.
    We’re summarizing categories, not numeric totals — a great fit for pie charts.

  • Clear proportions & Simplified categories.
    The focus is on how many countries fall into each participation group, not individual values. The use of three broad groups (Low, Medium, High) makes the chart easy to read and interpret.

  • Descriptive caption and title.
    Viewers know exactly what they’re looking at, what the levels mean, and what region/timeframe it covers.

  • Clear takeaway.
    More than 60% of Sub-Saharan African countries fall into the medium IGO participation level, making it the dominant category. The chart also clearly separates the other levels — this gives a quick, visual summary of how countries in the region are grouped by IGO participation.

3.3.1.3 Visualization is a support tool, not the conclusion

While this chart gives a helpful overview of IGO participation levels, it doesn’t tell the full story on its own. A pie chart like this shows proportions — but not which specific countries are in each category, what those IGOs are, or why participation levels differ.

To better understand regional participation patterns, pair this chart with information such as:

  • A list of Sub-Saharan African countries
  • Background on major IGOs active in the region

Together, these elements help turn the chart into a meaningful, evidence-based explanation.

3.3.3 Histogram — Visualizing Distributions

A histogram shows how continuous values are distributed across intervals (called bins). It helps us see the overall shape of a variable — for example, whether most countries have high, low, or mid-range scores.

What It Is

  • A histogram groups numeric values into equal intervals (bins) and counts how many observations fall in each range.
  • The height of each bar shows how many cases fall in that interval.

Type of Data

  • Continuous (interval or ratio) variables — e.g., democracy scores, GDP per capita, gender equality.

Purpose

  • To understand how values cluster or spread across their range.
  • To detect skewness, outliers, or bimodal patterns (two peaks).

3.3.3.1 Example with ggplot2: Gender Equality in the Asia-Pacific (2014)

Let’s look at how gender equality scores (v2x_gender) are distributed among countries in the Asia and Pacific region. This index measures how equally men and women enjoy civil liberties, access to power, and participation in society. Scores range from 0 to 1.

# Subset data for the Asia-Pacific region
data <- POLI_LIT %>%
  filter(e_regionpol_6C == 6, year == 2014)

# Create the histogram
ggplot(data, aes(x = v2x_gender)) +
  geom_histogram(binwidth = 0.05, fill = "steelblue", color = "white") +
   scale_x_continuous(breaks = seq(0, 1, by = 0.05)) +
  labs(
    title = "Distribution of Gender Equality Scores in Asia and Pacific (2014)",
    subtitle = "x = v2x_gender",
    x = "Gender Equality Score (0–1)",
    y = "Number of Countries"
  ) +
  theme_minimal()

# ggplot() starts the plot and defines what data/variables to use.
# aes() maps x = v2x_gender, the gender equality score.
# geom_histogram() creates bins (intervals) showing how many countries fall in each range.
# binwidth = 0.05 controls the size of each bin (smaller = more detailed).
# scale_x_continuous() sets consistent tick marks for easier reading.
# fill and color define bar color and outlines.
# labs() adds a title and axis labels.
# theme_minimal() simplifies the layout for readability.

3.3.3.2 How to Read This Chart

  • x-axis → v2x_gender (gender equality score, 0 = low equality, 1 = high equality).
  • y-axis → number of countries falling within each score range.
  • Interpretation:
    Countries in the Asia-Pacific region cluster around a moderate level of gender equality — with the tallest bar around the 0.65 range, representing about 5 countries. Only a handful of countries scored above 0.8, as shown by the short bars on the far right.

This histogram gives a quick visual sense of how gender equality varies across the region — and shows that very high scores are rare. This chart helps us see distribution patterns in the region.

3.3.4 Bar Chart — Comparing Category Averages

A bar chart compares numerical summaries — usually averages — across categories.
It’s one of the most common ways to show political or social differences between groups such as regions or years.

What It Is

  • A bar chart displays the average value of a numeric variable for each group.
  • It’s ideal for comparing regions, political systems, or other categorical divisions.

Type of Data

  • Categorical variable on the x-axis.
  • Numeric variable on the y-axis (mean or total).

Purpose

  • To compare average values between categories (e.g., democracy or gender equality by region).
  • To show which groups score higher or lower on a specific indicator.

3.3.4.1 Example with ggplot2: Average Gender Equality by Region (2014)

Let’s compare the average Gender Equality Index (v2x_gender) across world regions in 2014.

# Create a dataset grouped by region (mean gender equality per region)
data <- POLI_LIT %>%
  filter(year == 2014) %>%                    
  group_by(e_regionpol_6C) %>%     # group countries by region
  summarise(mean_gender = mean(v2x_gender, na.rm = TRUE)) %>%
  # Add readable region names
  mutate(region_name = case_when(
    e_regionpol_6C == 1 ~ "E. Europe & Central Asia",
    e_regionpol_6C == 2 ~ "Latin America & Caribbean",
    e_regionpol_6C == 3 ~ "Middle East & North Africa",
    e_regionpol_6C == 4 ~ "Sub-Saharan Africa",
    e_regionpol_6C == 5 ~ "W. Europe & N. America",
    e_regionpol_6C == 6 ~ "Asia & Pacific"
  ))

# Create the bar chart
ggplot(data, aes(x = reorder(region_name, mean_gender), y = mean_gender, fill = region_name)) +
  geom_bar(stat = "identity", color = "white") +
  labs(
    title = "Average Gender Equality by Region (2014)",
    subtitle = "Higher scores mean greater equality (0–1 scale)",
    x = "Region",
    y = "Mean Gender Equality Score",
    fill = "Region",
    caption = "Source: POLI_LIT_v1 (2025), V-Dem Codebook"
  ) +
  coord_flip() +  # horizontal bars for readability
  scale_fill_viridis_d(option = "cividis", guide = "none") +
  theme_minimal()

# aes() maps variables: x = region name (ordered by value), y = average gender score, fill = region color.
# geom_bar(stat = "identity") creates bars based on actual values (not counts).
# color = "white" adds a clean outline to each bar.
# labs() adds the title, subtitle, axis labels, legend title, and a data source caption.
# coord_flip() rotates the bar chart to horizontal for better readability.
# scale_fill_viridis_d() uses a color-blind-friendly palette.
# guide = "none" hides the redundant legend since region names are already on the axis.
# theme_minimal() applies a clean and simple visual style to the chart.

3.3.4.2 How to Read This Chart

  • x-axis → e_regionpol_6C (region codes).
  • y-axis → mean_gender: average Gender Equality Index in 2014
  • Interpretation:
    Regions like Western Europe & North America show the highest average scores — indicating more equal access and representation across genders. This bar chart helps you quickly compare levels of gender equality across global regions in a single year.

3.3.4.4 Histogram vs. Bar Chart — What’s the Difference?

Although they may look similar, histograms and bar charts display different types of data:

  • Histogram → continuous (numeric) data Groups numbers into intervals (bins) to show distributions.
    Example: GDP per capita — how many countries fall within each income range.
    Bars touch because data are continuous.

  • Bar Chart → categorical data Compares averages or totals across categories.
    Example: average gender equality by region.
    Bars are separated by space because categories are distinct.

🔍 In short:
A histogram shows the shape of a distribution,
while a bar chart compares values across groups.

3.3.5 Line Plot — Change Over Time

A line plot connects points across time to show how values change.
It helps us see long-term trends, such as growth, decline, or stability in political, economic, or social indicators.

What It Is

  • A line plot displays data points connected by lines.
  • Each point represents a year (x-axis) and the value of a variable (y-axis).

Type of Data

  • Time-series data — a numeric variable that changes across years.

Purpose

  • To show changes or trends over time.
  • Useful for tracking patterns such as increases, declines, or fluctuations.
  • Common for political, economic, and social indicators (e.g., democracy, GDP, IGO participation).

3.3.9 Scatter Plot — Exploring Relationships

A scatter plot shows how two numeric variables relate to each other.
Each dot represents one observation (for example, one country), placed according to two values: one on the x-axis and one on the y-axis.

What It Is

A scatter plot positions points on a grid — one variable on the x-axis, one on the y-axis — to show patterns, clusters, or relationships between them.

Type of Data

  • Two continuous (numeric) variables, such as democracy, GDP, or equality measures.

Purpose

  • To examine whether two variables move together.
  • To identify correlations — positive, negative, or none.
  • To spot outliers or unusual cases that don’t follow the general trend.

3.3.10 Example: Democracy and Censorship (2014)

Let’s explore the relationship between democracy (v2x_polyarchy) and Government social media censorship in practice (v2smgovsmcenprc) in 2014. v2smgovsmcenprc measures the degree government censor political content (i.e., deleting or filtering specific posts for political reasons) on social media in practice.

# Subset data for 2014
data <- POLI_LIT %>%
  filter(year == 2014)

# Create the scatter plot
ggplot(data, aes(x = v2x_polyarchy, y = v2smgovsmcenprc)) +
  geom_point( size = 3, alpha = 0.7) +   ## plots one point per country
  geom_smooth(method = "lm") +
  labs(
    title = "Relationship Between Democracy and social media censorship (2014)",
    subtitle = "x = v2x_polyarchy, y = v2smgovsmcenprc",
    x = "Democracy Score (0–1)",
    y = "Government social media censorship (+/- 4)"
  ) +
  theme_minimal()
## `geom_smooth()` using formula = 'y ~ x'

# ggplot() starts the plot.
# aes() maps x = democracy score, y = state monopoly score.
# geom_point() plots dots for each country.
# geom_smooth(method = "lm") adds a linear trend line (best-fit line).
# se = TRUE adds a shaded confidence band around the line.
# labs() provides clear labels for interpretation.
# theme_minimal() removes unnecessary gridlines for clarity.

3.3.10.1 How to Read This Chart

  • x-axis → v2x_polyarchy (democracy score).
  • y-axis → v2smgovsmcenprc (Government social media censorship in practice).
  • Interpretation:
    Each dot = one country in 2014.
    The line shows the general relationship between democracy and censorship degree. If the line slopes upward, it suggests that more democratic countries tend to have less political censorship on social media (higher values on the censorship variable = less censorship).

🔍 The line in this plot comes from geom_smooth(method = “lm”), which fits a linear regression model to the data. This is a statistical “best‑fit” line that summarizes the overall trend between the two variables. It does not connect the dots — it estimates the pattern.

3.4 Summary: Choosing the Right Plot Type

Now that we’ve explored different kinds of plots, let’s step back and summarize what we learned. Each plot type serves a specific purpose — it helps us see something different in our data.

Think of choosing a plot type like choosing the right tool for a job:
you wouldn’t use a hammer to screw in a bolt, and you wouldn’t use a pie chart to show change over time.

Use the table below as a guide to help you decide which type of chart to use in your own analysis.

Plot Type Kind of Variable(s) When to Use It Example (Practice Dataset) Example (University Life)
Pie Chart 1 categorical variable Use a pie chart when you want to show proportions — how parts make up a whole. Each slice represents one category’s share of the total. Share of countries by region (e_regionpol_6C) Share of students by class year (Freshman, Sophomore, etc.)
Histogram 1 continuous variable Use a histogram to see the distribution of values — how data are spread out (highs, lows, clusters, or outliers). GDP per capita (e_gdppc) — shows how countries differ in income levels. Distribution of students’ study hours per week.
Bar Chart 1 categorical + 1 numeric variable Use a bar chart to compare values or averages across categories. Each bar shows a group’s average score or total. Average democracy index (v2x_polyarchy) by region. Average GPA by major.
Line Plot 1 time variable + 1 continuous variable Use a line plot to show change over time — increases, decreases, or stable patterns across years. India’s democracy score (v2x_polyarchy) from 2004–2014. Your coffee spending over the semester.
Scatter Plot 2 continuous variables Use a scatter plot to explore relationships or correlations — whether two things move together, or not at all. Democracy (v2x_polyarchy) vs. Gender equality (v2x_gender). Study hours vs. grades.

3.4.1 Recap: Good Visualization

  • Different plots answer different questions.
    A bar chart answers “Which group is larger?”, while a line plot answers “How did this change over time?”

  • Categorical vs. Continuous:
    Categorical data (like region or gender) fits best in bar or pie charts.
    Continuous data (like GDP or democracy scores) fits better in histograms or scatter plots.

  • Change vs. Comparison:
    If you’re looking at change across years, use a line plot.
    If you’re comparing groups at one point in time, use a bar chart.

  • Show patterns, not every number.
    Visualization is about making the story easier to see — the goal is clarity, not decoration.

  • Always include context.
    Add clear titles, axis labels, and a source note so your reader knows what the data represent.

  • Simplicity is power. You don’t need fancy effects to make a good plot — the clearest plots are often the best ones.

4.1.2 Tips for Reading and Making Visualizations

Before you start, take a few minutes to plan your visualization.
Think about what story or pattern you want to show, and what kind of chart best fits your data.

It can help to start by asking:

  • What question do I want my chart to answer?
  • What type of plot show that pattern most clearly?

Below are some guidelines that will help you both read and create visualizations effectively.

When Reading a Visualization When Making a Visualization
1. Identify what’s being compared. Look at the axes, labels, or colors — what variables are shown? Choose clear variables. Make sure your x- and y-axes (or categories) represent what you want to show.
2. Look for overall patterns. Do values increase, decrease, or form clusters? Are there any outliers? Highlight key patterns. Use color, grouping, or labels to guide the viewer’s eye to the main trend.
3. Notice variation. Are data tightly clustered or widely spread? What does that say about consistency or diversity? Show variation clearly. Use a proper scale and avoid distorting differences with uneven axes.
4. Check context. What time period, region, or dataset is being shown? Label clearly. Always include axis labels, units, and a clear title that explains what the plot shows.
5. Ask critical questions. What does the chart say clearly, and what might be missing? Be honest and transparent. Avoid cherry-picking data, hiding missing values, or exaggerating small effects.
Make it accessible. Choose readable colors, avoid clutter, and include a short note about your data source.
